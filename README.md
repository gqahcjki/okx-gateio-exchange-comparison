## What a trade actually costs, not just the headline rate

On a $1,000 spot trade, taker fees are identical: $1.00 on each. The maker difference is real but small — $0.80 on OKX against $1.00 on Gate, so a limit-order trader doing 100 round trips a month of $1,000 each saves roughly $40.

For futures, a $10,000 position opened and closed with market orders costs about $10.00 on both at 0.05% taker. Neither venue has an edge there.

Two things the headline rate hides.

**European fee schedules differ from the global site.** OKX applies a higher spot schedule in the EEA than on its global page. If you're in the EU, compare your local fee page before you assume 0.08%/0.10% applies to you. This is the single most common way fee comparisons go wrong for European readers.

**Small caps carry a second cost.** Gate's order books on obscure listings are thin, which means wider spreads. You pay the 0.10% fee plus whatever the spread costs you, and on a thin book that spread can easily exceed the fee. The real cost of trading an illiquid coin on Gate often runs above the advertised rate. OKX takes the opposite trade-off: fewer assets, deeper books.

Both exchanges let you cut fees further by holding their token — OKB on OKX, GT on Gate.

## Safety, licences, and how each proves reserves

Neither exchange has an unblemished or a disastrous record. Both are imperfect in different ways.

Gate's predecessor Bter lost roughly 7,170 BTC in 2015. A 2019 Ethereum Classic attack hit the exchange, and Gate covered user losses. Nothing major has followed since.

OKX has operated since 2017 without a major loss of user funds. It did pay a large US settlement in 2025 over past compliance failures, and that settlement opened the door to its licensed US spot entity.

On licences, both claim eight. OKX's list includes MiCA and MiFID II in Europe, MAS in Singapore (MPI), AUSTRAC in Australia, and VARA in the UAE. Gate's includes AUSTRAC, a Hong Kong TCSP, a Gibraltar DLT provider registration, Malta VFA/MiCA, Bahamas SCB, Japan FSA through Gate Japan, and Cyprus CySEC. Both hold MiCA authorisation through Malta, so for EU readers both are inside the same framework.

Proof of reserves differs in a way that matters if you plan to verify it yourself:

- **Gate:** Merkle tree plus zk-SNARKs, implementation audited by Hacken.
- **OKX:** monthly zk-STARK/Merkle attestation, self-verifiable, with no named auditor disclosed.

Self-verifiable is a real feature — you can check your own balance is included — but an unnamed auditor means you're trusting the exchange's own process rather than a third party's signature.

Most people don't. One main account covers spot and derivatives on majors. A second account earns its place only when a specific asset isn't listed where you primarily trade.
